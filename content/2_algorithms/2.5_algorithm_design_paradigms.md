# Algorithm and Design Paradigm

## Introduction

In note 2.3 you met several step-by-step recipes (called **algorithms**) for two jobs: searching and sorting. Looking
back,
some of those recipes shared the same *way of thinking*. Linear search simply tried everything. Selection sort kept
grabbing "the best item right now". Merge sort and quick sort split a big job into smaller copies of itself. A reusable
way of thinking like this is called a **design paradigm**: a general *strategy* for inventing algorithms. This note is
about those strategies, how to tell whether a recipe built from one of them is correct, and how to choose between them.

### What is a design paradigm?

An algorithm solves **one** problem. A paradigm is a plan of attack that works on **many** problems. Think of cooking,
where roasting, boiling and frying are *techniques*, and a recipe for one particular dish uses one or more of them. Once
you know the techniques, you can cook dishes you have never seen before.

| Paradigm            | Everyday picture                                     | The idea in one phrase                                                                  |
|:--------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Brute force         | Trying every key on a big key ring                   | Try every possibility                                                                   |
| Backtracking        | Exploring a maze and walking back from dead ends     | Build the answer step by step, undo a step when stuck                                   |
| Greedy              | Grabbing the biggest banknote first                  | Take the best-looking choice now and never look back                                    |
| Dynamic programming | Keeping a notebook of answers you already worked out | Solve each small piece once, then reuse the answer                                      |
| Divide and conquer  | Splitting a pile of exams between helpers            | Split, solve the pieces separately, combine (see merge sort and quick sort in note 2.3) |

### Why are they important?

- **A few ideas solve hundreds of problems.** Learning the strategy is worth far more than memorizing single recipes.
- **The strategy decides whether you finish.** For the same problem, one strategy may need a split second and another
  may need longer than the age of the universe (section B shows how).
- **The strategy tells you how to check your answer.** Each paradigm comes with its own way of convincing yourself that
  the recipe is correct for *every* input, not just the ones you tried.

## A. Design and proof toolkit

Before meeting the strategies themselves, we need a few **habits** that apply to every strategy. They answer two
questions: *"How do I design a recipe on purpose?"* and *"How do I know it is right?"*

### The four-step habit: specify, design, prove, analyze

| Step       | The question to ask                                                | Example: "find the largest number in a list"                                                               |
|:-----------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 1. Specify | Exactly what goes in, and what must come out?                      | In: a list with at least one number. Out: a number from the list that is at least as big as all the others |
| 2. Design  | Which strategy? What are the steps?                                | Look at the numbers one by one and remember the biggest seen so far                                        |
| 3. Prove   | Why is the output right for **every** input, not just my examples? | The "biggest so far" is always the biggest of the numbers looked at so far (see Tool 1 below)              |
| 4. Analyze | How much work and memory does it need?                             | One look per number, one number remembered: work in proportion to n, $O(n)$                                |

**Why prove at all?** Trying a few examples shows that a recipe *can* work, not that it *always* works. A recipe can
pass
hundreds of tests and still fail on one unusual input. Binary search (note 2.3) is a real example: the idea was
published in 1946, yet a version that worked for every list size took until 1962, and a flaw was still found in a
widely used library in 2006.

#### Step 1 in detail: writing a precise problem statement

A vague problem cannot be solved or checked. A precise one names three things:

- **Input**: what the recipe is given (and what is *allowed*, for example "a list of at least one number").
- **Output**: what it must produce.
- **Rules**: what makes an output acceptable, or what makes one output better than another.

```text
  Vague:     "Find the best route."
 
  Precise:   Input:   a road map with the length of every road, a start town and an end town.
             Output:  a route from the start to the end.
             Rule:    among all such routes, the total length must be as small as possible.
```

Always ask about the **edge cases** too: an empty list, a list with one item, repeated values, input that is already
sorted, input that is in reverse. Many wrong recipes fail on exactly these.

### Tool 1: Loop invariant (a promise that stays true every round)

A **loop** repeats the same steps again and again. Each repetition is a **round** (a *pass*, in the language of note
2.3). A **loop invariant** is a statement about the work done so far that

1. is true **before** the first round,
2. is still true **after every round**, and
3. when the loop stops, **proves the answer is right**. ("Invariant" means "does not change": the *statement* stays true
   even though the data keeps changing.)

**Picture.** Sorting playing cards in your hand (insertion sort in note 2.3). The promise is: *"the cards in my left
hand are in order."* It is true at the start (one card is always in order), it stays true every time you slide the next
card into its place, and when no cards are left in your right hand, the promise says *all* cards are in order.

| Checkpoint | The question                                           | Card-sorting example                                             |
|:-----------|:-------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Start      | Is the promise true before the first round?            | One card in the left hand is in order                            |
| Keep       | If it is true before a round, is it true after?        | Sliding the next card into the right spot keeps the order        |
| End        | When the loop stops, does the promise give the answer? | No cards are left in the right hand, so all of them are in order |

> **Key idea.** A loop invariant lets you check a loop **without** running it on every input. You check three small
> things (start, keep, end), and together they cover every possible input.

### Tool 2: Induction (the domino argument)

Many recipes are **recursive**: they hand smaller copies of the same job back to themselves (as merge sort does). To
prove such a recipe correct, we use **induction**, which works like a line of dominoes.

1. **Base case:** show the recipe works for the smallest input. (The first domino falls.)
2. **Step:** show that *if* the recipe works for all smaller inputs, then it works for this one. (Each domino knocks
   over the next.)
   Then it works for **every** size.

```text
  [first domino falls]  ->  [2nd]  ->  [3rd]  ->  [4th]  ->  ...  all fall
   base case                 each one knocks over the next (the step)
```

**Example: why merge sort is correct.**

- *Base case:* a list with one item is already sorted.
- *Step:* when sorting a bigger list, each half is smaller, so we may **assume** the recipe already sorts it
  correctly. Merging two sorted halves gives a sorted whole (that is exactly what the merge step does).
- So merge sort is correct for 1 item, then for 2 (two halves of 1), then for 3 and 4 (halves of at most 2), and so on
  for every size.

> **Key idea.** Assuming "the smaller copies are right" is not cheating. It is the previous domino, which has already
> been shown to fall.

### Tool 3: Exchange argument (swapping toward the recipe's answer)

This is the main tool for proving that a **greedy** recipe is right (section C uses it a lot). The plan:

1. Take **any** best answer, even one produced by somebody else's recipe.
2. Show that you can **swap** one piece of it for the piece *our* recipe would choose, **without making it worse**.
3. Repeat. Step by step, the best answer turns into our recipe's answer, so ours is just as good. **Example: serving
   customers one at a time.** Customer A needs 5 minutes, customer B needs 2 minutes. Everyone wants to
   be finished as early as possible (shortest waiting + processing time), so we add up the moments at which the
   customers finish.

| Order     | A finishes at | B finishes at | Total |
|:----------|:--------------|:--------------|:------|
| A, then B | 5             | 7             | 12    |
| B, then A | 7             | 2             | 9     |

Swapping the pair so that the **shorter** job goes first never hurts: the pair as a whole still ends at the same
moment (so nobody after them is affected), and the short job finishes sooner. Repeating such swaps (just like bubble
sort fixing out-of-order neighbours in note 2.3) turns *any* order into "shortest job first" without ever making the
total worse. So "shortest job first" is best.

### Tool 4: Counterexample (one input that breaks the recipe)

To show a recipe is **wrong**, a single failing input is enough. That input is called a **counterexample**.

- Claim: "to find the smallest item, look only at the first and last items." Counterexample: `5  1  9`. The recipe says
  5,
  but the smallest item is 1.
- From note 2.3: binary search on the **unsorted** list `20  50  10  40  30` wrongly answers "not found" for 20.
  Where to hunt for counterexamples: tiny inputs, an empty input, one item, repeated values, already sorted, reverse
  order, and extreme values.

> **Key idea.** Proof and counterexample are two sides of one coin. If you cannot prove a recipe, try to break it. If
> you cannot break it after an honest search, go back and try to prove it.

### Which tool answers which question?

| The question                                        | The tool                                                                                              |
|:----------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Does my loop do the right thing, round after round? | Loop invariant                                                                                        |
| Is my recursive recipe correct for every size?      | Induction                                                                                             |
| Is my "take the best choice now" rule really best?  | Exchange argument (or "greedy stays ahead")                                                           |
| Is my recipe wrong?                                 | Counterexample                                                                                        |
| How much work does it need?                         | Count the levels times the work per level, or the number of subproblems times the work per subproblem |

### Worked example: the four steps on "find the largest number"

List: `4  9  2  7`. The invariant (our promise) is: *"the number I am remembering is the biggest among the numbers I
have looked at so far."*

```text
  Before any look:   nothing seen yet; start by remembering the first number
  Look 1:  see 4     remembered = 4     (biggest of: 4)
  Look 2:  see 9     9 > 4, so remembered = 9     (biggest of: 4 9)
  Look 3:  see 2     2 < 9, keep 9                (biggest of: 4 9 2)
  Look 4:  see 7     7 < 9, keep 9                (biggest of: 4 9 2 7)
  List finished -> the promise now says: 9 is the biggest of ALL the numbers.  Correct.
```

- **Start:** after looking at just the first number, it is the biggest of one number.
- **Keep:** a new number either beats the remembered one (we replace it) or does not (we keep it); either way the
  promise stays true.
- **End:** when the list is finished, "biggest of the numbers looked at" means "biggest of all numbers".
- **Analysis:** one look per number, so work grows in proportion to n, $O (n)$, and only one number is remembered.

> #### Quick Learning Check 1
> (a) Someone claims: "to find the smallest item, look only at the first and last items." Find a counterexample.
> (b) A loop adds up the numbers of a list one by one. State a loop invariant for it.
> (c) Which tool would you use to show that a recursive recipe works for lists of every size?
>
> **Solution.**
> (a) Take `5  1  9`. The first item is 5 and the last is 9, so the claim answers 5, but the smallest item is 1 (in
> the middle).
> (b) "The running total always equals the sum of the numbers looked at so far." It is true at the start (nothing
> looked at, total 0), stays true after each number is added, and at the end the total is the sum of all numbers.
> (c) **Induction**: show the smallest case works, then show that "all smaller cases work" makes this case work.

## B. Brute force and backtracking

Some problems have no known clever shortcut. They ask for a valid (or the best) arrangement among a huge number of
possibilities. The most basic strategy is to look at **every** possibility. A smarter version, **backtracking**, still
looks at the possibilities in an organized way, but stops exploring a path the moment it becomes hopeless.

### Brute force (exhaustive search)

#### The problem

Find an answer by examining **all possible candidates** and keeping the one (s) that follow the rules (or the best one).
For example, which of these numbers add up to exactly 12? In which order should a van visit five houses so that the trip
is shortest? Which groups of friends can share one car?

#### The core idea: _Generate every candidate, check each one_

This is what you do when you try every key on a big key ring. It is **always correct**, as long as you truly cover every
candidate. The only question is *how many candidates there are*.

#### How it works

1. List every candidate in an organized way, so that none is missed and none is repeated.
2. Check each candidate against the rules.
3. Keep the valid one (s), or the best one.

> **Key idea.** Brute force is the simplest recipe and the hardest one to get wrong. Its cost is *the number of
> candidates* times *the cost of checking one*. It is also a handy **referee**: to test a clever recipe, run it and
> brute force on many small inputs and compare the answers.

#### Recursion trees: seeing all the candidates at once

A candidate is usually built by a series of **choices**. Draw those choices as a tree (a **recursion tree**), every
level is one decision, every branch is one option, and every path from the top to the bottom is one complete candidate.

**Subsets.** For the items `a`, `b`, `c`, each item is either taken (left branch) or skipped (right branch).

```text
                                       start
                               /                   \
                    {a}                                     {}
                /         \                             /         \
         {a,b}                {a}                 {b}                 {}
        /    \              /    \              /    \              /    \
   {a,b,c}    {a,b}     {a,c}      {a}      {b,c}      {b}       {c}       {}
```

Each of the 8 bottom nodes is one possible group. With 3 items there are 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 groups, so with n items there
are $2^n$ groups.

**Orderings.** For the same three items there are 3 choices for the first place, then 2 for the second, then 1:

```text
  first    second    third
  a        b         c        -> abc
           c         b        -> acb
  b        a         c        -> bac
           c         a        -> bca
  c        a         b        -> cab
           b         a        -> cba
```

3 x 2 x 1 = 6 orderings. With n items there are n x (n - 1) x ... x 1, written $n!$ (n factorial").

| Items (n) | Groups (subsets), $2^n$ | Orderings, $n!$                |
|:----------|:------------------------|:-------------------------------|
| 3         | 8                       | 6                              |
| 5         | 32                      | 120                            |
| 10        | 1,024                   | 3,628,800                      |
| 20        | 1,048,576               | about 2.4 quintillion          |
| 30        | about 1.07 billion      | about 265 followed by 30 zeros |

#### Worked example: which numbers add up to 12?

The numbers are `3  5  7  9`. Brute force looks at all 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 = 16 groups and adds each one up:

```text
  {}         0        {3,5}      8        {3,5,7}   15        {3,5,7,9}  24
  {3}        3        {3,7}     10        {3,5,9}   17
  {5}        5        {3,9}     12  <--   {3,7,9}   19
  {7}        7        {5,7}     12  <--   {5,7,9}   21
  {9}        9        {5,9}     14
                      {7,9}     16
```

Two groups work: `{3, 9}` and `{5, 7}`. With 4 numbers this is trivial. With 40 numbers there would be about a trillion
groups (1,099,511,627,776), which is a very different story.

#### Cost: why exhaustive searches blow up

To see *why* the cost explodes, **unroll** the recipe level by level. Solving the problem for n items means making one
decision (take or skip) and then solving the *same* problem for n - 1 items, **twice**: once for each option. So every
extra item **doubles** the work.

| Decisions made so far | Paths in the tree |
|:----------------------|:------------------|
| 0                     | 1                 |
| 1                     | 2                 |
| 2                     | 4                 |
| 3                     | 8                 |
| 4                     | 16                |
| ...                   | ...               |
| n                     | $2^n$             |

Compare this with merge sort. Both recipes split a job in two. The difference is *how much the job
shrinks*:

|                    | Merge sort                                         | Take-or-skip search (subsets)                      |
|:-------------------|:---------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------|
| What one step does | Cuts the list into two pieces of **half** the size | Makes two branches, each only **one item** smaller |
| Number of levels   | about $\log n$                                     | n                                                  |
| Work on each level | about n in total (it stays level)                  | doubles from level to level                        |
| Total work         | $O(n \log n)$                                      | $O(2^n)$                                           |

Halving shrinks a problem very fast. Removing a single item at a time does not.

How bad is it? Suppose a computer performs **a billion steps per second** (rounded, for illustration):

| Items (n) | Groups, $2^n$ steps            | Orderings, $n!$ steps                                                    |
|:----------|:-------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 10        | about a millionth of a second  | about 4 thousandths of a second                                          |
| 20        | about a thousandth of a second | about **77 years**                                                       |
| 30        | about 1 second                 | about 8 quadrillion years (the universe is about 13.8 billion years old) |

Doubling still creeps up on you: n = 40 takes about 18 minutes, n = 50 about 13 days, n = 60 about 36 years.

> **Key idea.** Brute force is fine for small inputs: roughly up to 20 or 25 items for groups, and only about 10 for
> orderings. Beyond that, we need a smarter strategy.

> #### Quick Learning Check 2
> (a) In how many different orders can 4 books stand on a shelf?
> (b) A pizza shop offers 5 toppings. How many different topping choices are there, counting "no toppings" and
> "all five"?
> (c) One more topping is added to the menu. What happens to the number of choices?
>
> **Solution.** (a) 4 x 3 x 2 x 1 = **24**. (b) Each topping is "on" or "off": 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 = **32**. (c) It
> **doubles** to 64. That doubling is exactly what makes $O (2^n)$ grow so fast.

### Backtracking

#### The problem

Brute force wastes huge effort on candidates that were doomed after their first few choices. If two queens on a
chessboard already attack each other, there is no point in placing the other six.

The classic example is the **n-queens puzzle**: place n chess queens on an n x n board so that **no two attack each
other**. A queen attacks along its row, its column and both diagonals.

The 8-queens puzzle has 4,426,165,368 ways to put 8 queens on 8 of the 64 squares, but only **92** of them are valid.
The puzzle was published by the chess composer Max Bezzel in 1848, and Franz Nauck published the first solutions in
1850 (and extended the puzzle to n queens).

#### The core idea: _Build the answer one choice at a time, check the rules after every choice, and walk back the moment a path is hopeless_

Think of exploring a maze. At every junction you pick a corridor. If you reach a dead end, you walk back to the last
junction and try the next corridor.

Three words to know:

- A **partial answer** is a half-built candidate (for example, 3 queens placed out of 8).
- **Pruning** means cutting off a whole branch of the tree because its partial answer already breaks a rule. None of
  the (possibly millions of) candidates below it needs to be looked at.
- To **backtrack** is to undo the latest choice and try the next option for that step.

#### How it works

1. **Make** the next choice, which extends the partial answer.
2. **Check the rules.** If the partial answer already breaks one, **prune**: undo the choice and try the next option for
   this step.
3. If all choices are made and the rules still hold, you have found a **solution**. Record it (and stop, or keep going
   to find all of them).
4. If a step has no options left, undo the **previous** choice and try *its* next option.

> **Key idea.** The only thing that must be proved about a pruning rule is that it **never cuts off a path that could
> still lead to a valid answer**. A rule that prunes too eagerly makes the recipe wrong, and a rule that prunes too
> little only makes it slow.

#### Worked example

Suppose you need to choose 2 numbers from {1, 2, 3}, and their sum must equal 4.

We can build our answer one number at a time:
```53s5
Start
├── Choose 1
│    ├── Choose 1 → 1 + 1 = 2 (not enough)
│    ├── Choose 2 → 1 + 2 = 3 (not enough)
│    └── Choose 3 → 1 + 3 = 4 ✓ SOLUTION
│
├── Choose 2
│    └── Choose 2 → 2 + 2 = 4 ✓ SOLUTION
│
└── Choose 3
    └── No valid combination (the 3 + 1 is already found, and 3 + 2 is too big)
```

#### Complexity

- **Worst case:** the *label* can stay as bad as brute force, such as $O (n!)$ for queens or $O (2^n)$ for groups,
  because
  for some inputs pruning cuts very little. In practice, though, the real work is often thousands or millions of times
  smaller.
- **Memory:** very little. Backtracking remembers only the **current path** (like a hiker unrolling a thread through the
  maze), so the memory grows with the number of choices, not with the number of candidates.
- **One solution or all?** Stopping at the first solution can be much faster than collecting every solution.

#### Strengths and limits

- It is the general tool for **puzzles with rules**: Sudoku, crosswords, timetables with constraints, arranging guests
  who must not sit together.
- **Check the rules as early as possible.** The earlier a bad partial answer is rejected, the more of the tree is
  pruned.
- It is not magic. Some problems stay out of reach even with clever pruning, a topic that returns in section H.
- Brute force also has a dark side: **password guessing**. Each extra character in a password multiplies the number of
  guesses an attacker must try, which is why long passwords are strong.

> #### Quick Learning Check 3
> (a) In "which numbers add up to 12?" with only **positive** numbers, we prune a path as soon as its running total goes
> above 12. Why would this rule be **wrong** if negative numbers were allowed?
> (b) In the 4-queens trace, why was row 3 a dead end after queens at (1,1) and (2,3)?
>
> **Solution.**
> (a) With negative numbers, a total above 12 can come back down when a negative number is added later. Pruning would
> cut off a path that could still succeed, and a pruning rule must **never** do that.
> (b) Every square in row 3 is attacked: column 1 by the queen in row 1 (same column), column 3 by the queen in row 2
> (same column), and columns 2 and 4 by the diagonals of the queen in row 2. No queen can go in row 3, so the search
> must backtrack.
